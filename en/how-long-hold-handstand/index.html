How long should you hold a handstand? Benchmarks by level

You're holding 4 seconds and wondering if that's any good. Short answer: if you hold 4 freestanding seconds, you're already in a small minority of the population. Here are the complete reference points to place your level and know what to aim for next.

The benchmark table

Freestanding hold timeLevelWhat it means
1 to 3 secondsAdvanced beginnerYou're finding vertical: the hardest part is done
5 to 10 secondsIntermediateYour fingers correct actively, balance is no longer luck
15 to 30 secondsSolidConsolidated position, you can breathe upside down
30 to 60 secondsAdvancedEnough to start press work and variations
60 seconds and moreVery advancedThe fruit of regular, dedicated practice, achievable in 1 to 2 years for a committed athlete

Four clarifications to read this table honestly:

We're talking best hold, not average. The universal standard is the best time of the session. Your average hold will always be much lower, and that's normal.

Wall time doesn't count here. A 60-second chest-to-wall hold is an excellent training milestone, but the benchmarks above are for freestanding balance only.

The line matters as much as the time. 10 seconds in a banana is worth less than 6 seconds aligned: the banana burns energy and plateaus quickly. (How to fix the banana)

These numbers refer to the full hold, feet together from start to finish, which is the measure people generally mean when they quote a time. The split freestanding hold, one leg forward and one back, is still worth training for its own sake: the centre of gravity sits lower, which makes it excellent ground for learning to control your balance. Tracking your records on that variation alongside the full hold gives a truer picture of your progress, and that is what Handstand Pro lets you do.

Why each milestone is easier than the last

Counter-intuitive but verified by nearly every practitioner: going from 0 to 5 seconds often takes longer than going from 5 to 15.

The explanation is the role of the fingers. Under 5 seconds, you're surviving your kick-up: the hold lasts as long as luck allows. Beyond that, your fingers have learned active braking and every drift is corrected before it becomes a fall. Balance stops being an event and becomes a maintained state. Typical learning timelines for each milestone are detailed in How long does it take to learn a handstand?

How to measure properly (the protocol)

Comparable numbers demand a constant protocol:

  1. The clock starts when your feet leave the floor and your body passes vertical.
  2. The clock stops when the first foot touches back down.
  3. Count the best hold of the session, across 10 to 15 attempts maximum (beyond that, fatigue skews everything).
  4. Log it, every session. An unrecorded time is a lost time: handstand progress only shows across weeks of data.

Timing yourself upside down isn't easy: film yourself, or use the built-in timer in Handstand Pro to record your best times, session after session.

What about records?

For perspective: static handstand world records run into tens of minutes, and professional circus hand balancers work multi-minute holds in varied shapes. In other words, the room for progress is endless, and a 30-second hold already places you very, very far along the distribution.

FAQ

What's the average person's time?
Zero seconds: the vast majority of adults have never held a freestanding handstand. From your very first held second, you're an outlier.

My record is 3 weeks old, should I worry?
No. Best times progress in jumps, not linearly. Watch the average of your 3 best weekly holds instead: that's what should climb slowly.

How many seconds before working on the one-arm handstand?
The commonly accepted standard: a comfortable, repeatable 60-second two-arm hold before seriously starting one-arm weight transfers.
