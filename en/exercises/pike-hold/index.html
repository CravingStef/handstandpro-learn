Pike hold

Hands and feet on the floor, hips as high as you can get them, and you walk your hands in toward your feet until your arms start to work. From the side it draws an upside-down V closing in. This is where the whole pike family starts, and the only one that needs neither a wall nor a box.

The goal isn't to touch your feet. Every centimeter you gain lifts the hips and pours weight into your hands, until your shoulders carry nearly all of it. Same lesson as at the wall, less impressive to look at, and you can do it in a hallway.

How to do it

  1. Hands and feet on the floor, hips high, back long.
  2. Walk your hands in toward your feet in small steps, until the weight shifts forward.
  3. Straight arms, ears between them, push the floor away.
  4. Shift your shoulders over your hands: that's where the load lands.

The two mistakes that make it useless

Rounding your back to gain ground. The hamstrings pull, the pelvis tips back, the back curls, and the hips drop instead of rising. Bend your knees. A pike with bent knees and a straight back loads the shoulders better than a pike with straight legs and a rounded back.

Sinking between your arms. The elbows give, the head drops, and the position turns into a passive hold where the joints carry instead of the muscles. Push the floor away with straight arms, until you feel the space between your ears and your shoulders.

The one that survives my mornings

I train in the morning, before work, and my sessions run ten to twenty minutes depending on what the day looks like. The pike hold needs no equipment, and that's why it stays in my weeks when flashier drills get dropped.

Shoulder opening is still my limit, decent, but with room to improve, and it's exactly what this position works. You don't need an hour-long slot to start: two meters of clear floor is enough, and that's what makes it easy to come back to.
