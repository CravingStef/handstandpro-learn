Wall split-leg balance

You kick up with your back to the wall and your legs in a split: one leg goes toward the wall, the other stays in front. Only the toes of your back leg touch the wall, and you look for your balance in your fingers, leaning on the wall as little as you can.

The split is the easiest shape for finding your balance, because your center of gravity sits lower than with your feet together. It's the shape of the freestanding split handstand, with the wall behind you to catch anything that goes past vertical.

How to do it

  1. Back to the wall, hands on the floor about 30 cm from it, kick up and split your legs.
  2. Keep only the toes of your back leg on the wall, legs straight.
  3. Push tall and find your balance in your fingers: their pressure is what stops you from drifting toward the wall.
  4. Once the position holds, try toe taps against the wall during the hold. That's the alternating taps drill.
  5. Once you feel comfortable, place your hands further from the wall.

Two mistakes to avoid

Leaning on the wall. The foot against the wall takes some of your weight, and you're holding the position thanks to it without realizing it. Keep the contact light, just the tip of your toes, so your fingers do the work. To check, lift your toe a centimeter off the wall: if you tip toward the wall straight away, you were leaning on it.

Bending your legs. When the balance gets hard, your knees bend and the split closes up without you meaning to. Lock your legs straight and keep them apart, since that's what lowers your center of gravity and makes the position easier.

The same shape as my favorite drill

The freestanding split handstand is one of my two favorite drills for improving my max hold. I held 23 seconds freestanding, filmed in Corsica on 29 July 2026, then 57 seconds on 13 August, two weeks later.

If the freestanding split still feels far off, this is where you build the same shape, with the wall behind you. Once your toe is only brushing the wall, move your hands away, then try without it.
