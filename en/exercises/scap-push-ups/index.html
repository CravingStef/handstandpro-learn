Scap push-ups

A push-up where your arms don't bend. You're in a plank, arms straight, and only your shoulder blades move: they squeeze together, your chest drops a few centimeters between your shoulders, then you push the floor away until your upper back rounds. The range is small, and that's normal.

In a handstand, the second half of the movement is the one that counts. Pushing the floor away, shoulders toward your ears, locks your shoulder girdle and stacks the line, and your wrists take far less load. The problem is that upside down you can barely feel what your shoulder blades are doing. Here, right way up and on the floor, you learn to feel them.

How to do it

  1. Get into a plank, hands under your shoulders, arms locked.
  2. Without bending your elbows, let your shoulder blades squeeze together: your chest drops a little between your shoulders.
  3. Push the floor away and round your upper back, as far as you can.
  4. Come back slowly and repeat. The movement only comes from your shoulder blades.

The two mistakes that cancel it out

Bending your elbows. To get more range, your arms join in and the drill turns into a small ordinary push-up. Your shoulder blades then have nothing left to do. Keep your arms straight and accept a movement of a few centimeters.

Letting your hips sag. All your attention goes to your shoulders, and meanwhile your pelvis drops and your lower back arches. It's the same plank as usual: glutes squeezed, ribs in, pelvis tilted back.

The string between my shoulder blades

In a plank, I picture a string attached between my shoulder blades pulling me up toward the ceiling. The scap push-up is that string being pulled and released, one rep after another. I can hold about two minutes in a plank as I write this, at the end of October 2026, compared with a little over a minute in handstand, and when people ask me how, part of my answer is: breathe slowly, and push well through your shoulders, as in a scap push-up.

A coach in your ear

Upside down, you can't read a screen. In the app, a voice guides you through the exercise and tells you what to fix right when you need it. For example, when your elbows bend to go lower:

“Keep your arms straight throughout”
