Pike leg raises

Sitting on the floor, legs straight in front of you, hands beside your knees. You lift one leg, then the other, then both. From the outside it looks like nothing at all. And yet it's what decides whether you'll ever press to handstand, because it's the same movement, the other way round.

Compression, the word that comes up everywhere, means that and nothing more: bringing your chest toward your thighs with your belly, without pulling with your arms.

How to do it

  1. Sit in a pike, legs straight in front of you, back straight, hands by your knees.
  2. Start with one leg only, then the other, then both together.
  3. Bring your chest toward your thighs as you lift.
  4. Lower with control, without letting the leg drop.

The two mistakes that keep your legs on the floor

Pushing on your hands. Your arms push the floor away, your torso rises, and your legs come up by leverage. Your belly stops doing anything. Your hands are there to steady you, not to carry: keep their contact light.

Sitting with a rounded back. When your hamstrings pull, your pelvis tips back and your back rounds. From there your legs have no chance of leaving the floor. Sit on a folded cushion or a book to lift your pelvis, and straighten your back before you start.

What's left of six years of gymnastics

I did more than six years of club gymnastics, and what stayed with me isn't balance: we were only ever asked for three to five seconds on our hands. It's flexibility. It's exactly what helps here, and it's about the only place where my past gives me a head start on someone starting out. The good news is that flexibility responds well to training and improves little by little, whatever your age.

A coach in your ear

Upside down, you can't read a screen. In the app, a voice guides you through the exercise and tells you what to fix right when you need it. For example, when your legs barely leave the floor:
