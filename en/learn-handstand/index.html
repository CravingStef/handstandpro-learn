How to learn a handstand: the complete beginner's guide

Balancing on your hands is not reserved for gymnasts or for kids. With a progressive method, an adult beginner can learn the handstand in a few months, whatever their age or athletic background.

This guide covers the whole path: the prerequisites, the wall drills, the safe bail out, freestanding balance, realistic timelines by profile and the mistakes that cost months. The method is the one built into the Handstand Pro app, based on gymnastics fundamentals and tested on its author's own practice and on a growing group of users.

Who is the handstand for?

A quick word on vocabulary first, because three terms often get mixed up:

  • The headstand: balancing on your head, hands on the floor in a tripod. It is easier, and it is not a handstand.
  • The handstand: balancing on your hands only, arms straight, body aligned.
  • Hand balancing: the generic term, used in circus and yoga.

This guide is about the straight-arm handstand. Contrary to what people assume, it demands neither exceptional strength nor contortionist flexibility. If you can hold a plank for 30 seconds and raise your arms overhead without arching your lower back, you have what you need to start.

Age is not an obstacle either. The handstand is a skill of coordination and patience more than of power: plenty of practitioners learn it after 30, 40 or 50. We have a whole article on the question: Learning a handstand after 30 or 40: is it too late?

The 3 prerequisites before you start

1. Prepared wrists

Your wrists are about to carry your full bodyweight in maximal extension, a position they never meet in daily life. Five minutes of specific warm-up before every session prevents most of the pain. Full protocol here: Wrist and shoulder mobility for handstands

At the end of July 2026, a month and a half after my comeback, my right hand started to get seriously tired and I had to stop for a few days. It was entirely my own fault: I was rushing my warm-ups, or skipping them altogether, before filming a few attempts for stories. Since then, wrist rotations and flexions are systematic.

Stéphane Perrin, his story

2. Enough shoulder opening

Simple test: back against a wall, lower back flat against it, raise your arms straight overhead. If your hands touch the wall without your lower back lifting off, your shoulder opening is enough to start. If not, that is your priority, because a lack of shoulder opening is what creates the famous "banana" shape.

3. Basic core strength

The handstand is an upside-down plank. If you can hold a strict plank for 30 to 45 seconds, you have the core strength to begin. The rest builds itself through practice.

Step 1: build the position against the wall

The wall works at every level. Gymnasts use it throughout their whole career to work on alignment, and you keep coming back to it long after you have found the freestanding balance, because you cannot build a clean line while you spend your time catching your balance. Three fundamental drills:

The wall walk.
Start in a plank with your feet on the wall, then walk your feet up while bringing your hands closer. Stop as soon as you lose control of the position. The drill builds shoulder strength and the habit of being upside down, with no risk of falling.

The chest to wall handstand.
Same climb, but you finish as close to the wall as possible, chest facing it, only your toes in contact. This is the reference position: it forces a correct alignment, and the banana becomes impossible.

Toe taps, hands placed fairly far from the wall.
From a back to wall handstand, lift one foot then the other, alternating and looking for the balance for a few seconds before touching the wall again. Placing your hands further from the wall makes the drill much more demanding, and it is one of the best transfers to freestanding.

A measurable marker, not a permission slip: if you cannot hold a minute against the wall, you will not hold a minute freestanding. Your shoulders need to carry your bodyweight without shaking before you can expect a long hold. This is not a toll to pay before you are allowed to try freestanding, which you should attempt as soon as the bail out is acquired: it is the shoulder level that decides how long you will last once you are off the wall.

For my one-minute record, I trained for about three months, four to five sessions a week, with the emphasis on shoulder strength. The people around me noticed the muscle gain before I noticed my progress in balance.

Stéphane Perrin, his story

Step 2: learn to come down safely

This is the step almost everyone skips, and the reason so many practitioners stall at the wall for months.

It matters for two distinct reasons. The first is injury: among adult practitioners you regularly see brutal landings, with heavy impact on the toes and ankles, from people nobody ever taught to come down.

The second one unlocks your progress. As long as you cannot exit a failed handstand, you will not dare kick up with enough energy: you will systematically go a little too short, without understanding why, because a protective reflex holds you back. Once the bail out is automatic, you can deliberately go past vertical without consequence, and that is how you find the right dose.

The cartwheel bail out means pivoting on one hand and stepping your feet down to the side when you overbalance. One or two sessions are enough to automate it. Step by step tutorial: Overcoming the fear of falling: how to bail out of a handstand

Step 3: find the freestanding balance

Once the wall is under control and the bail out is automatic, freestanding balance rests on three things:

Your fingers are your brakes. Balance happens mostly in the pressure of your fingers on the floor, not only in the shoulders or the hips. If you tip over towards your back, you press down through your fingertips; if you fall back towards your feet, you push into the heel of your hand. It is the role your toes play when you stand up.

Which settles one thing about hand position: your fingers do not stay flat on the floor, they stay slightly curled, so that you can push through the tips. As for the rest, spacing, orientation, how wide you go, there are several schools and the right one depends on your morphology. Look for the position where your shoulders sit comfortably over your hands.

Your gaze rests between your hands, on the floor, slightly ahead of the line of your hands, neither on your hands nor far in front. That stabilises the inner ear without breaking neck alignment.

The kick up is dosed, not forced. Go up with less drive than feels necessary and adjust session after session. Too strong a kick ends in a cartwheel, which is no problem at all once step 2 is acquired; too short a kick is fixed by letting your shoulders travel slightly forward.

At this stage, an ideal session looks like this: wrist warm-up, 2 or 3 chest to wall holds, then 10 to 15 freestanding attempts, writing down your best time each session. That is exactly the cycle Handstand Pro times and records for you.

The real obstacle: sizing your corrections

The difficulty that holds out the longest is neither the banana, nor the fear of falling, nor the wrists. It is the size of your micro-corrections.

Here is what a badly sized correction looks like: you drift slightly towards your back, you press on your fingers to catch yourself, but you press too hard, so the body swings the other way, you correct again to catch the first correction, you swing back once more, and you end up on the floor after three growing oscillations. What was missing was the calibration of the movement, not the strength.
Think of the unconscious micro-corrections your feet and toes make when you are standing up!

This difficulty explains the rest of the method. If the obstacle is an automatism to build, then four short sessions beat one long session, and a program you follow beats practising when you feel like it. It is also why progress is measured in weeks rather than in sessions.

Finding the right amount of energy in the corrections is what took me the longest, and I am still working on it. It takes practice time for those adjustments to become almost unconscious.

Stéphane Perrin, his story

Step 4: hold it longer and longer

The first few times, you will hold 1 or 2 seconds. That is normal, and it is already a handstand.

The typical progression goes through plateaus: 2 seconds, then 5, then 10, then the symbolic 30-second mark. Each plateau asks for less and less luck and for more and more silent finger corrections. To see where you stand and what is realistic to aim for, check our benchmarks by level: How long should you hold a handstand? The benchmarks

One useful piece of advice: time every attempt. Handstand progress is invisible day to day and spectacular month to month. Without data, you will feel like you are stalling and you will quit at the exact moment it was about to click.

Film yourself too, from the side, and compare the reality with what you pictured. What you feel upside down almost never matches what the video shows, and that is extremely useful for knowing what to correct.

How often to train

Ideally 5 to 6 sessions a week of 15 to 20 minutes, with at least one rest day. That is the rhythm that progresses fastest, because a nervous-system skill is built through frequent repetition rather than through volume.

Depending on the time you have, a session runs from 10 to 20 minutes, and ten minutes is already a real session. Four to five sessions of 15 to 20 minutes give the best ratio of time invested to results. At three sessions a week, progress stays steady. At two, it is slower but it is real: people do learn at that rhythm, it simply takes them more months. What does not work is practising in bursts with no program, two hours one Sunday then nothing for ten days.

The mistakes that slow everyone down

  1. The banana, arched back and legs drifting behind. The cause is almost always insufficient shoulder opening, or shoulders that are not pushing enough. It gets fixed chest to wall, never freestanding. Keeping your legs firm helps too.
  2. Balancing with your hips. Big waves through the body mean your fingers are not working hard enough.
  3. Skipping the bail out, which condemns you to short kicks for months.
  4. Training tired, at the end of a workout, when the nervous system stops responding and stops learning.
  5. Practising in bursts, two hours one Sunday then nothing for ten days.

FAQ

How should I place my hands?
There are several schools on spacing and orientation, and the right placement depends on your morphology: look for the position where your shoulders sit comfortably over your hands. One point is not up for debate: your fingers do not stay flat on the floor, they stay slightly curled so you can push through the tips, since that is what you correct your balance with.

How do I manage to kick up?
By learning to come down first. As long as the cartwheel bail out is not automatic, a protective reflex will make you kick too softly and you will drop back without understanding why. Once the bail out is acquired, you can deliberately go past vertical a few times at no risk, and that is how you find the right dose.

Can anyone do a handstand?
Yes, with very different timelines depending on athletic background, flexibility and consistency. What usually stops people is not the body, it is quitting after three weeks.

Do I need to learn the headstand first?
No. The headstand, on your head, and the handstand, on your hands, are two different skills, and the headstand can even build alignment habits you will have to undo. Yoga inversions work on real things and are far from useless, they are simply not a step towards balancing on your hands.

Do I need to be flexible?
Only shoulder opening really counts. Flexibility in your legs or back has no effect on a straight handstand. Do the wall test described in the prerequisites.

Can I learn on my own, without a coach?
Yes, provided you have a coherent program and objective feedback on your position. Be persistent, and film yourself from the side every session: what you feel upside down is almost always different from reality. The Handstand Pro timer completes that feedback by keeping track of your best times.

Is the handstand dangerous for the back or the wrists?
Practised progressively, no. The two points to watch are warming up your wrists before every session and learning the bail out before attempting freestanding.

How many minutes a day should I practise?
10 to 20 minutes per session, ideally 5 to 6 times a week with a rest day, beats one long weekly session by a wide margin. The nervous system learns through frequent repetition, not through volume. Two or three sessions a week also work, progress is simply slower.
