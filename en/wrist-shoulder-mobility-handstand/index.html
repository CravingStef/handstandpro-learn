Wrist and shoulder mobility for handstands

I did five years of gymnastics as a kid and I never learned to properly hold a handstand. Nobody asked us to. Three to five seconds of passing through on the floor was the whole requirement.

I started again in mid-June 2026. Three weeks in I held 20 seconds. After six weeks, 46. And my shoulders are still the thing standing between me and a line I would be happy with, which is why the second half of this page exists.

The handstand asks for almost no flexibility. Two places, and that is it. The wrists carry your bodyweight in full extension, and they are where beginners feel pain. The shoulders decide whether your body stacks up straight or bends into the banana everyone recognises in their own photos.

Wrists first, because that is where it hurts

Why wrists hurt

Standing on your hands, your wrists are at roughly 90 degrees of extension under your full bodyweight. In daily life, they almost never exceed 60 degrees under light load. Beginner pain is usually not an injury: it's tissue discovering a new demand. It disappears with a systematic warm-up and gradual volume.

The warm-up protocol (5 minutes, before every session)

  1. Wrist circles: hands clasped, 20 circles each direction.
  2. Rocking on all fours, fingers forward: kneeling, hands on the floor, rock your bodyweight forward and back. 15 reps, going slightly further each time.
  3. Fingers toward you: same drill, hands flipped, fingers pointing at your knees. 10 gentle rocks. The most important stretch of the protocol.
  4. Palm lifts: on all fours, weight on your hands, lift your palms while keeping fingers on the floor. 10 reps. Strengthens the flexors that stabilize the wrist.
  5. Fingertip push-ups (optional, level 2): on all fours, shift from flat palm to fingertips. 5 reps.

The anti-pain rules

  • Spread the load: in a handstand, weight goes across the whole hand (knuckle line included), not just the heel of the palm.
  • Dose your volume: wrists strengthen more slowly than shoulders. If a session stings, cut upside-down time by 30% for a week rather than stopping cold.
  • Sharp, localized pain = stop: a diffuse warming discomfort is normal at first; a pinpoint pain in the same spot on every rep is not. In that case, see a professional.

Shoulders, the half of this page I had to rewrite

Someone on r/Handstands read this article and told me it had a wrist warm-up and nothing usable for the other big problem. He was right. The shoulder part was three lines at the bottom and nobody ever got that far. Here it is properly, and it is the half I need most myself.

The 30-second test that tells you whether this is your problem

Stand with your back against a wall, feet about 10 cm out, lower back flat against the wall. Now raise both arms overhead, elbows straight, and try to touch the wall with your thumbs.

Two outcomes.

Thumbs touch, lower back stays flat. Your shoulders are fine. Skip to the last section, this is not what is limiting you, and no amount of stretching will buy you seconds.

Your arms stop short, or your lower back peels off the wall to let them through. That is the whole problem, in one movement. Upside down your body will find the missing range somewhere, and it takes it from your lower back. The result is the banana. Most people go hunting for core strength at that point, which is why they stay bananas for a year: nothing in your abs can produce range your shoulders do not have. (The other classic mistakes)

Do the test in front of a mirror or film it. Everybody cheats on this one without noticing, usually by letting the ribs flare.

What it costs you upside down

A closed shoulder does not stop you doing a handstand. It stops you doing an easy one.

With the arms slightly in front of the body rather than in line with it, you are holding a small lever all the time. The hold becomes a strength exercise instead of a resting position, the wrists take more load, and the balance corrections have to be bigger. That is the difference between a 10-second fight and a 40-second hold that feels almost boring.

Three drills, and the one that actually did something for me

  1. Chest sink on a table or a chair. Kneel, elbows on the support, hands clasped behind your neck, let your chest fall between your arms. 3 sets of 45 seconds. If I had to keep one, this is it. It is also the one I quietly skip when I am short on time, which is probably why, at the time of writing, my shoulders are still the thing holding my line back.
  2. Passive hang from a bar. 2 sets of 30 seconds, shoulders relaxed, nothing to do but breathe. It decompresses and opens at once, and it costs nothing.
  3. Wall slides. Back to the wall, arms sliding up and down with elbows and wrists in contact. 2 sets of 10, slowly. Least spectacular of the three, but it teaches you where the range actually stops.

Three times a week is enough. Every day is not better here: this is connective tissue, it changes on its own schedule.

Give it 4 to 8 weeks before you retest. In the meantime, keep working chest-to-wall, which physically prevents you from arching and lets you build the hold while the range catches up (the 5 wall drills).

Not everyone needs this

Worth saying plainly, because mobility work has become a religion. Plenty of people pass the wall test on the first try and never think about their shoulders again. If that is you, the time is better spent on kick-ups.

But if you failed it, this is not one item on a list of ten. It is the first thing, and it gates everything after it.

The push that lightens your wrists

One last thing, usually discovered far too late. In a handstand you do not just stand on your arms. You push the floor away, actively, until your shoulders travel up toward your ears.

It sounds like a detail and it changes the whole position. The shoulder girdle locks, the line stacks, and the wrists suddenly carry less. If your wrists are complaining, try this before you try anything else.

To learn the movement: in a straight-arm plank, push the floor hard enough to round your upper back, release, repeat 10 times. Same gesture, right way up.

FAQ

Can I train with wrist pain?
With diffuse early-practice discomfort, yes, reducing volume and being diligent with the warm-up. With sharp, localized, reproducible pain, no: take a week off and consult if it persists.

Are wrist wraps useful?
Support wraps can help occasionally on high-volume days, but they don't replace gradual tissue adaptation. Use as a supplement, not a foundation.

Should I stretch after the session?
The fingers-toward-you rocks (exercise 3) in a gentle version, 1 minute, are enough. The essential work happens before the session, not after.

My shoulders are tight. Should I wait until they open before training handstands?
No. Train chest-to-wall while you work on the range: the wall does the job your shoulders cannot do yet, and you keep building the hold instead of waiting for permission. What you should not do is chase freestanding time with closed shoulders and then blame your core.
