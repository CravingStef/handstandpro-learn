Handstand vs headstand: what's the difference?

"I can do a handstand" often turns out to mean a headstand, and the confusion causes endless misunderstandings in gyms, yoga studios and social media comments. This lexicon puts every term in its place, ranks the positions by difficulty, and answers the question that always follows: which one should you start with?

The lexicon, position by position

The headstand

The crown of the head and both hands (or forearms) form a tripod on the floor. The support base is wide and the center of gravity is low: it's the most accessible inversion, holdable within a few sessions for most people. In yoga it's Sirsasana. Careful: accessible doesn't mean harmless, since the neck is loaded.

The tripod headstand

A headstand variant where the knees rest on the elbows before extending up. Often the very first inversion taught to kids and beginner yogis.

The handstand

Both hands on the floor, arms locked, body in one line from head to toes. In yoga it appears as Adho Mukha Vrksasana; in gymnastics it's simply the handstand. The support base shrinks to two hands and the center of gravity is high: balance is actively maintained through finger pressure. It's the skill covered in our complete guide.

The advanced variants (to map what comes next)

The press handstand (straight-leg entry, no momentum), the one-arm handstand, and shapes (straddle, tuck, flag) belong to the next chapter of the discipline, once the straight handstand is held for 30 to 60 seconds (the benchmarks by level).

Should you learn the headstand before the handstand?

No, and it's the most widespread misconception on the subject. The two positions share being upside down, and almost nothing else:

  • Balance doesn't live in the same place: on the head and forearms for the headstand, in the fingers for the handstand.
  • Alignment differs: the headstand tolerates (and encourages) an arch that the handstand forbids.
  • Transfer is nearly zero, and sometimes negative: months of headstands install hip-balancing reflexes you'll have to unlearn.

If your goal is the handstand, start directly with the wall work: it's safe, more effective, and every minute counts toward the right movement. The headstand remains a fine position to practice for its own sake, notably in yoga, but as a separate discipline.

Why so much confusion between the two?

Because everyday language grabbed "handstand" as a catch-all for anything upside down, while yoga popularized the headstand as the "king of asanas", making it the inversion most people have actually seen. The result is two overlapping vocabularies (casual and technical). In practice, remember this: on the head = headstand, on the hands with straight arms = handstand.

FAQ

Is the headstand dangerous for the neck?
Practiced with good technique and a prepared neck, it's widely done in yoga without problems. But any neck pain means stopping immediately, and anyone with a history of cervical issues should consult before trying.

Is a yoga handstand (Adho Mukha Vrksasana) the same as a gymnastics handstand?
The target position is the same. The traditions differ on the path: yoga often kicks up near a wall with breathwork, the gymnastics method prioritizes chest-to-wall alignment and a dosed kick-up. Our method follows the gymnastics route, the most direct path to freestanding balance.

Does the forearm stand fit in this list?
The forearm stand (Pincha Mayurasana) sits between the two: forearms on the floor, more stable than a handstand, no load on the neck. A fine intermediate goal, but like the headstand, its transfer to the handstand is limited.
